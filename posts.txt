Yes. What you're describing is possible, but there is an important distinction: no normal storage medium can guarantee that a photo will remain readable forever. You can, however, choose media designed for very long-term, offline preservation.

If you mean something like a game cartridge—you write the data once, put it somewhere safe, don't power it for years, and later plug it in and read it—the closest options are:

🥇 Archival optical disc — M-DISC

M-DISC is probably the closest match to what you're imagining.

Data is physically etched into an archival recording layer.

No electricity is required while it's stored.

Resistant to many environmental effects compared with ordinary recordable discs.

Designed specifically for long-term archival storage.

You can store a photo on it and put the disc away.

You still need a compatible optical drive decades later.


Think of it as:

Write once → remove it → store safely → read many years later.

For one extremely important photo, I'd actually make multiple copies rather than trusting one disc.

🥈 Archival-grade SD/flash — not my first choice

Flash memory can behave like a cartridge, but it isn't ideal for decades of unattended storage. The charge representing the data can gradually deteriorate, especially with heat.

So:

Game cartridge feeling: ✅
Best possible long-term archive: ❌

🥉 Hard drive

A hard drive is excellent for storing huge amounts of data cheaply, but it's not really a "forever cartridge."

A powered-off HDD can survive many years, but:

mechanical parts can fail

bearings can seize

magnetic data can eventually deteriorate

finding compatible hardware decades later becomes harder


For your particular situation, I wouldn't make one HDD containing the only copy of the photo.

⭐ Something even more interesting: glass archival storage

There are technologies that encode data into glass/quartz. These are much closer to your idea of a physical object that can preserve information for extremely long periods.

For example, Microsoft's Project Silica demonstrated storing information in quartz glass.

The catch: this isn't currently a practical consumer storage product you can simply buy in Bangladesh and put your photo on.


---

If the photo is genuinely irreplaceable

I'd use a 3-layer preservation strategy:

Photo → M-DISC copy #1
Photo → M-DISC copy #2
Photo → HDD copy

Then physically store the two discs in separate safe locations.

And don't just save the original JPEG. Keep:

the original photo

a high-quality PNG/TIFF archival copy

a text file containing information about the photo

optionally a checksum (e.g. SHA-256) so you can later verify that the file hasn't changed


That gives you something much closer to a "real-life cartridge for your photo."

If you want, I can also explain which physical storage medium has the best realistic lifespan—10, 50, 100, 500+ years—and what you could actually buy in Bangladesh on a low budget.

Yes — if the platters are physically intact, there is a real chance of recovering the data, even if the HDD itself is completely undetectable.

From what you described:

❌ HDD is not recognized

❌ Motor/spindle isn't working

❌ Drive electronics/mechanics aren't functioning

✅ Platters appear physically intact

✅ No obvious cracks/scratches on the platters


That is actually a much better situation than having scratched or badly damaged platters.

The important part

The data isn't stored in the motor, PCB, heads, or spindle. The actual magnetic data is on the platters.

So if the platters are still readable, a professional recovery lab can potentially:

Dead HDD → repair/replace failed components → get platters spinning → read the magnetic data → make a sector-by-sector image → recover your files.

Professional recovery labs in Bangladesh specifically advertise recovery from HDDs that are not detected, mechanically failed, or have failed motors/heads/PCBs, using specialized hardware and cleanroom procedures. 

⚠️ Don't try to open it yourself

This is extremely important.

If the platters are intact, don't open the HDD cover at home just to check them. Even tiny airborne particles can contaminate the head/platter interface, and further powering a mechanically damaged drive can make recovery harder. Professional labs use controlled environments for this kind of work. 

If you already opened it, don't touch the platter surfaces and don't power it again.

If you mean how many years a normal HDD can actually last, there is no exact fixed lifetime.

Typical HDD lifespan

HDD condition	Typical usable life

Heavy 24/7 use	3–5 years
Normal home use	5–8 years
Light/intermittent use	7–10+ years
Well-maintained HDD that gets lucky	10–15+ years
Any HDD	Can fail at any time


For your storage-PC plan, because you said the computer will run roughly 6 hours/day rather than 24/7, that's generally easier on the drives than continuous operation.

But age isn't the only factor. A 2-year-old HDD with bad sectors can be more dangerous than a healthy 8-year-old HDD.

For your RAID storage

I'd use this rule:

0–3 years: generally keep using if SMART health is good.

3–5 years: monitor SMART closely.

5–7 years: consider replacing proactively, especially if the drive contains important data.

7+ years: I'd treat it as an aging drive and keep an independent backup.

Any warning signs: replace immediately, regardless of age.


And importantly, RAID doesn't increase an individual HDD's lifetime. It only helps keep your storage running when a drive fails.

For your planned 2TB used HDD RAID, I'd strongly recommend testing every used drive with a full surface scan + SMART data before trusting it with your 5TB collection.
